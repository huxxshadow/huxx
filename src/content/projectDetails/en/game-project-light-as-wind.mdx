## The Game

*Light as Wind* is a pixel-art side-scrolling adventure. Three plush toys – a starfish, a swordfish and a crab – go to see the sea for a sleeping girl, in a dream: from the pitch-dark deep through glowing gardens, a harp theatre, the open surface and the shore, chasing a faraway sky. The story comes in two parts; the current version is Part I, *Beneath the Blue*.

> They say that where the sea ends, there is a sky of deep blue.
>
> Every dream ends in waking. But not yet: the journey isn't over.
>
> So, before you wake, go a little further.

## My Role

Five of us from USC Games teamed up for That Game Jam and made this in about four weeks. I directed it and did the game design, lead programming and technical art; about nine in ten of the project's roughly five thousand commits are mine. In detail:

* **Procedural creatures**: the three playable plush heroes and dozens of creatures and plants – jellyfish, an anglerfish, sardine schools, a manta, seabirds, seagrass – all driven by code, with no keyframed animation.
* **A pixel render pipeline of my own**: it replaced URP – a low-resolution G-buffer, deferred toon lighting, light from glowing things, glass refraction and order-independent transparency, and one final quantisation to pixels.
* **Levels and world**: the level tools for ten levels (three depth layers, brush dressing, scene marks), background loading between levels, and the cutscene system.
* **Development tooling**: the Unity editor driven from the command line, with recording, stills, regression and tuning spread over several headless editors in parallel; performance logs from players' machines and automatic quality.
* **Direction and design**: the emotional arc of the whole game and the play of each level; I also made the trailer and the official website.

## Every One of Them Is Alive

There is not a single hand-keyed frame of animation in the game. Every character is split into the same layers: a brain decides what it wants, an intent turns that into targets, a control layer works out how the body moves, a physics layer handles contact and weight, and a skin layer rebuilds the mesh every frame. That is how the same starfish can walk, run and jump, reach out to grip a jellyfish's tentacle, or pull a plant out by the roots.

### The starfish: where the feet land

Each of the starfish's five arms is an independent leg. I built it a gait recorder that draws, frame by frame, whether each foot is standing, stepping or chasing its target. In the first version, running, the feet spent half their time chasing and slid 1.04 m for every metre walked; with the target pinned the moment a foot lands and the step easing halved, they slide 67 mm per metre and chase only 2.3% of the time.

### Jellyfish: a swarm that swims for itself

The jellyfish swarm surges upward like a school of fish, but each one moves by its own pulse: the bell tilts toward where it wants to go, contracts to jet forward, glides and sinks, then beats again. The flocking only gives them a direction; the route is drawn in the dressing tools. Another kind links up into a chain that spirals down from above and settles next to the starfish.

## A Pixel Render Pipeline of My Own

The game was first drawn with URP, but its 8-bit picture could not hold colours above 1 or real glow, dark water banded, the picture's alpha was taken up by pixel motion, and every new shader had to wire up parallax, pixel motion and depth all over again. So I wrote my own pipeline, **ProcAnimRP**, and removed URP entirely. Its core does not depend on Unity, and a frame is described by a frame graph:

1. the game camera draws into a **low-resolution G-buffer** (base colour, octahedron-encoded normals, linear depth, object IDs);
2. **deferred toon lighting**, water, fog, outlines and depth cues are all computed at that resolution, in HDR internally;
3. one last step **quantises it to the pixel picture**, which is then scaled up to the screen by a whole number – so the pixels stay square and crisp.

<figure class="law-narrow">
    <Image src={imgViews} alt="Six debug views of the new pipeline" widths={[640, 1220]} sizes="(min-width: 1024px) 736px, 100vw" />
    <figcaption>Six debug views from when the pipeline was first wired up: camera picture, base colour, normals, linear depth, geometry generated on the GPU by a compute shader, and the taken-over picture</figcaption>
</figure>

Everything added on top first had to match the old picture pixel for pixel, and only then gain new effects:

* **Tiled deferred point lights**: 100 lights scattered across the view cost 1.28 ms of GPU time for the whole frame.
* **Glowing things light their surroundings**: jellyfish, salps and vents cast their own colours on the sand and on their tentacles; the hero carries a real light, and grass and tube worms cast side-on 2D shadows along the ground.
* **Glass**: a quantised Fresnel rim around each jellyfish bell, the oral arms inside offset by whole texels to read as refraction; overlapping glass uses weighted blended order-independent transparency (OIT).

Pixel art's worst enemy is shimmer: when the camera moves less than a pixel per frame, edges jump back and forth between two pixels. I record the sub-pixel remainder of each object's motion separately and move whole cells in compositing, and every kind of asset is measured with a diagnostic shot that counts flickering pixels frame by frame. That is how the plants' shadows were caught: their shadow pass used the wrong view matrix, and fixing it cut the shimmer in the kelp-forest stretch by 61%.

## Levels, Cutscenes and the Climax

The levels scroll sideways, but the world is 3D: every level has three depth layers – front, middle and back – and the hero walks the middle one. I made a "level hub" in the editor: the brush can paint plants and props into a single layer, and the scene marks the crab's walkable route, the strips that must stay flat and the game camera's framing. Our designers and artists dressed the levels there.

## Tools That Let Five People Finish in Four Weeks

To finish ten levels in four weeks, I handed most of the checking and producing to tools:

* **The editor driven from the command line**: compiling, stills, per-frame motion data and video recording are each one command, talking to the editor through file requests; when the editor stalls, it says why.
* **Headless editors in parallel**: long jobs go to whichever headless editor is free; when tuning, several versions of the same change run on several editors at once, and one round of side-by-side stills and numbers takes about 40 seconds. I also pushed different modules forward at the same time in several parallel working copies (together with AI coding assistants), merging them back into the main line every day.
* **Flight recordings and regression**: every frame's colliders, contacts, logs and probes can be recorded and replayed; regression checks are tiered by speed and cached, and reference pictures are only recorded after I sign off on them.
* **Performance on players' machines**: the shipped game can keep a performance log, and the logs sent back turn into reports – that is how the hitches at the seams between levels were measured and removed. "Auto" quality in the settings picks a tier from the graphics card.
