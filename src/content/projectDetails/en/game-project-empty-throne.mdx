## My Role

*Empty Throne* is a music-puzzle narrative game completed by a **5-person team**, in which I held the most central multiple roles: **lead programmer, lead designer, project manager, and publishing lead**. Throughout the project cycle, I was responsible not only for the design and development of key gameplay and systems, but also led team collaboration, version advancement, and the complete launch and release process.

My main responsibilities included:

* **Core Gameplay Design and System Implementation**: Led the design and implementation of the game's core mechanics, including the "Magic Mirror truth revelation" system, dialogue-based deduction puzzles, and rhythm performance gameplay, enabling narrative, interaction, and puzzles to form a unified experience loop.
* **Technical Architecture and Toolchain Setup**: Independently built the project's main technical framework in Unity, developing an extensible dialogue system, modular narrative scripting structure, localization support framework, and a data-driven toolchain for rhythm performance.
* **Project Management and Cross-Functional Collaboration**: As project manager, responsible for task breakdown, milestone definition, development pacing, and coordinating communication and collaboration among design, art, music, and programming team members to ensure stable iteration toward project goals.
* **Publishing and Launch Operations**: Responsible for the Steam platform release process, including store page configuration, build packaging, version uploading, patch management, and post-launch maintenance, while also participating in promotional content production and community outreach to drive the project's commercial release.

## Project Details

*Empty Throne* is the first indie game I participated in and brought to an official Steam release — a genuine practice of the complete production cycle from "concept to commercial release." The game was developed in **Unity**, with an overall genre of **narrative-driven point-and-click puzzle game**, incorporating **rhythm interaction gameplay** and **dark fairy-tale narrative**. Players explore a bizarre, reinterpreted fairy-tale world through clicking, environmental investigation, character dialogue, and musical interaction, gradually uncovering the truth behind the story.

This project holds great personal significance. It was not only a creative breakthrough but a complete industrial practice. We did not simply finish a course-style demo — we truly continued to polish the project, improve content, fix issues, and ultimately published it on Steam. This meant I had to face not just "can the feature be implemented?" but the higher standard of "is the game complete, accessible, stable, and acceptable to real players?"

### Background: From Competition Concept to Official Release

*Empty Throne* originated from a small collaborative project I initiated with several friends in our spare time. In its earliest stage, the project was aimed at being a competition entry — hoping to complete a game prototype with a distinctive style around "narrative, music, and puzzle fusion." Although the work did not advance to the final round of the competition, all team members held strong confidence in its creative potential and emotional expressiveness, so we decided not to abandon it and continued to invest time in systematic refinement and expansion.

It was through this process of transitioning from "competition prototype" to "complete game" that the project gradually moved beyond a short-term creative experiment and evolved into an indie game truly targeting the player market. We continuously iterated on narrative structure, gameplay experience, audiovisual style, interaction pacing, and content completeness, accumulating extensive real-world experience in team collaboration, version management, player feedback, and launch preparation. This process gave me a genuine understanding that indie game development is not just about creating "interesting features," but an ongoing balancing act between creativity, technology, resources, and time.

### Core Gameplay: Merging Point-and-Click Puzzles with Rhythm Interaction

The core experience of *Empty Throne* is built on two parallel and mutually supportive design tracks: one centered on **exploration, dialogue, and clue-based deduction as point-and-click puzzle gameplay**, and the other on **music interaction that advances emotional expression and narrative revelation through rhythm and melody**. Rather than relying solely on text narrative or traditional item-based puzzles, we wanted players to not just "read the story" but truly "participate in the story" through action, judgment, and rhythmic perception.

My key design question was: how can narrative be not just passively presented, but converted into player behavior through mechanics? The project gradually formed several core interaction pillars.

First is the **Magic Mirror Truth System**. This mechanism is one of the most representative designs in the entire project. Players do not receive complete answers directly — they must observe, engage in dialogue, and trigger specific conditions to let "hidden truths" gradually emerge. Compared to traditional notebook-style clue accumulation, this system has stronger thematic consistency: the mirror itself is an important image in fairy-tale contexts, and naturally suits the role of "revealing reality / distorting truth" as an interactive medium. It makes puzzle mechanics not purely functional, but part of narrative expression.

Second is **dialogue-based deduction puzzles**. Dialogue in this game is not merely a story playback tool — it carries the core role of information filtering, character judgment, and clue assembly. In the system design, I aimed to make dialogue branches carry stronger logical meaning, enabling players to discover character contradictions, lies, and emotional shifts beneath the surface of language, then integrate these fragments through reasoning. This approach ties text content more tightly to the gameplay itself and increases the sense of participation in narrative interaction.

The third component is **musical performance and rhythm gameplay**. The rhythm system in this project is not an isolated mini-game insertion, but a key structural element serving atmosphere, plot, and character expression. We wanted music to be not merely background, but a means of "triggering story" and "completing puzzles." In the design, I bound rhythm gameplay to specific narrative segments, so that when players follow the beat, complete a melody, or hit key nodes, they receive clear operational feedback while simultaneously experiencing plot progression and emotional release.

### Inspirations and Early Design Exploration

As the project concept gradually took shape, we referenced a number of works with distinctive design languages, though the focus was not to copy existing forms but to distill design principles genuinely worth learning. We paid attention to how narrative-heavy works use text branching to build character depth, and observed how music rhythm games establish emotional pacing through clean, intuitive input feedback. We wanted the final result to not be a simple combination, but a new experience that naturally holds up within a "dark fairy-tale narrative" context.

Early in the project, we held multiple rounds of brainstorming around the core concept. Rather than immediately entering production, the team spent considerable time discussing: what emotions do we want players to experience? Should this world's tone lean more toward eerie, sorrowful, or oppressive? Is music a "decoration" or a "structure"? Should puzzles emphasize logical solutions or emotional resonance? These early discussions helped us gradually establish a shared creative direction and laid a unified foundation for the subsequent rapid prototype development.

### Prototype Phase: Rapidly Validating the Dialogue and Rhythm Systems

After establishing the mixed gameplay direction of "narrative puzzles + music rhythm," I took on the primary prototype development work and, as the sole core programmer in the project, quickly built the initial version. The focus of this stage was not art completion, but validating two critical questions:

1. Can the dialogue system support complex narrative expression and branching choices?
2. Can the rhythm system integrate naturally with the story presentation, rather than feeling awkwardly disconnected?

To this end, I prioritized building a basic dialogue framework, branching logic, and simple UI structure, while simultaneously implementing rhythm input judgment, timing detection, and feedback flow. The dialogue system was designed with an emphasis on character voice, information layering, and the progressive revelation of "hidden truth"; the rhythm system focused more on visual clarity, input feel, and integration with narrative nodes. Although this stage was still quite rough, it very effectively validated the most critical gameplay viability, giving the team clear grounding and direction for subsequent development.

### Full Development: From Gameplay Prototype to Complete Production Framework

As the project moved from concept validation to full development, my work shifted from "build a core prototype" to "construct a complete, sustainably productive system framework." Because team members were responsible for producing content in different directions, the code structure had to be well-extensible and collaboration-friendly, or subsequent content integration would become very difficult.

During this phase, I built and refined multiple core systems:

#### 1. Modular Dialogue and Narrative Scripting System

To accommodate large volumes of narrative text, character interactions, and branching flows, I designed a modular dialogue system supporting plot advancement, conditional branching, choice feedback, and event triggering. The system handles not just standard dialogue display but also links to scene state, character performance, and puzzle progress, allowing narrative content to integrate more naturally into the level flow.

#### 2. Rhythm Gameplay System

Rhythm gameplay is another technically complex system in the project. I implemented a complete runtime logic covering **beat judgment, input detection, combo tracking, and performance feedback**, allowing certain rhythm nodes to bind animation conditions or special performance logic, making the gameplay more than just "keeping the beat" — it serves specific contexts and narrative emotions. To facilitate future expansion, I also structured rhythm data as configurable as possible, making it easier for the team to add content or adjust difficulty.

#### 3. Animation Event and Performance Trigger System

To enhance the expressiveness of rhythm segments and narrative sequences, I developed a flexible animation event system allowing character actions, sound effects, visual feedback, or UI responses to be bound to specific timing points. This allowed designers and content members to fine-tune performance pacing without frequently modifying underlying logic.

#### 4. Music and Background Audio Management System

Given this game's heavy dependence on atmosphere, I implemented a music and ambient audio management module supporting track switching, fade in/out, configurable playback, and save-state linking. This ensures emotional continuity during narrative progression and keeps audio presentation stable and controllable across different scenes and events.

#### 5. Localization and Content Extension Support

To support both Chinese and English content, I built a cross-language text organization structure enabling UI, dialogue content, and rhythm-related text to be uniformly managed and switched. This not only laid the groundwork for subsequent publishing and distribution but also improved the overall maintainability of the project's content.

#### 6. Custom Editor Tools

To improve the team's efficiency in content configuration, I also wrote a number of editor tools for importing CSV data, managing rhythm nodes, and configuring animation events. For a small team, these tools may not be the most directly "player-visible" part, but they significantly improved production efficiency, reduced manual data-entry errors, and made subsequent iteration much smoother.

### First Playable Demo: Validating the Complete Experience Loop

After a period of focused development, we completed the project's first playable demo. This version allowed the team to experience the game's core flow from start to finish for the first time — from click-based exploration and dialogue advancement to rhythm interaction and basic narrative performance — with the overall loop taking initial shape. Although certain important features were still missing at the time, such as a complete save system, localization support, and a formal start screen, this demo was sufficient to prove that the project's gameplay direction was viable and that the combination of narrative and music mechanics genuinely offered unique experiential value.

The significance of this stage was that it moved the team from "an imagined game" to "a game that can be tested and discussed." Many things that seemed reasonable in design documents only revealed their problems once placed into an actual flow. From this point forward, the project's focus gradually shifted from "get it built" to "make it better."

### Bug Fixes and System Optimization: Gameplay Restructuring and Experience Upgrade

After the first demo was complete, we entered a prolonged phase of fixing and optimization. This stage was not just about clearing bugs — more importantly, it involved reviewing the overall design and rethinking which mechanics truly worked and which expressions were still insufficiently precise.

The most critical adjustment was restructuring the original "notebook-style" clue revelation approach into the current **Magic Mirror revelation mechanic**. This change greatly improved the consistency between gameplay expression and art narrative, making "discovering the truth" not just logically valid but more persuasive at the emotional and world-building level. At the same time, we supplemented and optimized the save/load system, background music system, and some rhythm chapter logic structures.

Particularly in the rhythm design of Acts 1 and 4, we made numerous adjustments targeting player input burden, perceived feedback latency, operational complexity, and visual information clarity. The goal was not simply to lower difficulty, but to make the rhythm gameplay serve the narrative experience more naturally rather than overshadowing it. Through these changes, the project achieved noticeable improvements in rhythmic feel, fluidity, and immersion.

### Alpha / Beta Testing: From Internal Validation to Player Feedback

As the project approached launch standards, we began organizing Alpha and Beta testing phases. To handle feedback more systematically, I established and promoted a Google Sheets-based bug submission and tracking workflow, enabling the team to more efficiently record issues, prioritize them, and synchronize fix progress. For small-team projects, the most common testing problem is not "bugs aren't being found" but "bugs are found but not effectively managed" — so establishing a structured feedback mechanism was very important.

During this phase, we also invited friends and early testers to playtest, observing how they understood the interface, systems, and narrative clues without any development background. This external feedback was invaluable, helping us identify many problems that the development team had grown accustomed to and could no longer see — such as certain UI hints being unclear, some rhythm feedback having too steep a comprehension curve, and a few puzzles not conveying information clearly enough. The iteration during testing significantly improved the game's readability and completeness, preparing it for launch.

### Launch and Publishing: From Developer to Publisher

As the project entered its final release phase, I took on the primary responsibilities for publishing and launch. This part of the work is fundamentally different from pure development, but equally important. I handled store page configuration, game build packaging and uploading, version distribution management, and post-launch patch maintenance through **Steamworks**, ensuring the game could be released to real players in a stable, presentable, and distributable form.

At the same time, I participated in and led the project's promotional efforts, including content publishing, pre-launch outreach, and asset organization on **Twitter, Bilibili, and Discord**. We produced and distributed promotional artwork, demo videos, and social media content to attract player interest. Throughout the launch and distribution period, the project accumulated over **20,000** platform views and impressions.

The game officially launched on Steam on **September 17, 2025**. For our small team, this represented not just the completion of a project, but the genuine conversion of a concept born from passion and enthusiasm into a commercial product — publicly released and entering players' libraries. Even more encouraging: in the first week after launch, the game entered the libraries of over **10,000** players. This result far exceeded our initial expectations and reaffirmed our belief that the emotional expression and narrative approach at the heart of this project had genuinely reached players.

From code implementation to mechanic design, from project management to Steam publishing, this project covers nearly every core dimension of my current game development capability. It gave me solid experience in Unity engineering architecture, narrative systems, rhythm interaction, and tool development, and deepened my understanding of the real-world complexity of indie game production. To me, *Empty Throne* is not just "the first released game" — it is a critical milestone proving that I can turn ideas into complete products.
