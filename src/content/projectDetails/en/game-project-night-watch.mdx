## My Role

Night Watch is an experimental project I ran at NetEase, focused on AI-native gameplay and AI game-development workflows: when a model can take part in producing content, what gameplay structure and development process fit it. My main responsibilities:

* **AI-Native Gameplay Design**: Building on the model's ability to generate content at runtime, unified characters, equipment, materials, locations, events and statuses into cards, reducing how much new content depends on the interface; designed random encounters as risky choices that don't directly reveal their outcome, making text variation the core playable variable.
* **LLM Runtime Content Pipeline**: Designed the generation protocol for event and enemy cards, covering structured output, consistency checks, a content whitelist, level ranges, tone and anti-spoiler constraints, a second-pass review, caching and timeout fallbacks — "the model handles creativity, the code handles rules and numbers."
* **AI Collaboration and Content Integration**: Set the writing-tone and art guidelines and built an AI-generation plus human-review workflow spanning code, art, music and script; unified the 80 card assets into three size tiers and integrated the full content into a React, Zustand and Three.js front end.

## Project Details

*Night Watch* is a Victorian-gothic **AI-native card roguelike**. Its core idea is to build a card framework that stays intuitive to play while carrying enough systemic depth, and then — through strictly defined content, numeric and rule constraints — let AI-generated content participate reliably in the random expansion that happens inside a run. That gives the game extensive meta progression and in-run build variation, and reaches a genuinely near-infinite space of builds that a traditional roguelike cannot.

The game is built for the web. You play a lamp-carrying watchman who boards a dangerous train and pushes forward through damp corridors, first-class compartments, the dining car, the boiler room and carriages swallowed by fog, looking for a way to survive one fight after another.

For its gameplay framework, *Night Watch* draws on the card interaction design of *Stacklands* and *Cultist Simulator*, abstracting characters, equipment, materials, locations, events and statuses into a single kind of draggable card object. One operation — dragging a card into the use slot — covers crafting, equipping, entering a location, and using an item.

### Everything Is a Card

There is no separate inventory screen, forge screen, or map screen — which action unlocks when a card enters the slot depends only on the card's own type:

* Material → **Craft** (complete a recipe and forge a piece of gear);
* Equipment → **Equip** (into main hand, off hand, head, body, hands, and two trinket slots);
* Location → **Enter** (treat that carriage card as a dungeon run);
* Consumable → **Use** (instant recovery, such as a medkit).

### Combat

Combat is an automatic turn-based system: gear takes effect on its own, the player can't act during a fight and can only hold to speed it up. This isn't a simplification but a deliberate move of decisions up front — a fight is often won or lost before you step into the carriage.

The core contest is between damage types and resistances. The game has blunt, blade and firearm damage, plus light, which counters non-human enemies; enemies fall into two groups:

* **Human enemies** (the drenched man, the lurking killer, the armed gunman, the mangled mechanic) are usually weak to blades and firearms, and being wet or mechanised adds an extra weakness to fire.
* **Non-human enemies** (whisperers, fog wraiths, the thing that should not exist, the Fog Sovereign) strongly resist ordinary physical attacks and fog damage, and need light, silver or fire to be countered.

So every location card is essentially a loadout puzzle: before entering "Uncanny Whispers" you might bring a silver cross and a storm lantern; heading into the "Machine Room", a gear cudgel and a pipe wrench work better. The cost of a gear choice doesn't show up in split-second play — it shapes the outcome from the first turn of the fight.

### Crafting, Affixes and Salvage: Letting Failure Add Up

Materials form the core of the progression loop. Thirteen of them — gears, silver, gunpowder, fog essence and more — are spread across different carriages, steering players to pick their route by what they're after.

Crafted gear gains a random prefix affix by rarity, such as Keen, Silver-Inlaid or Fog-Etched, so the same recipe can produce different results. Gear you don't need can be salvaged back into materials and fed into the next craft.

### Dungeon Structure: Binary Choices and Hidden Outcomes

Inside a location card, every step is a choice between two encounters, combat or non-combat (scavenging, traps, shrine offerings, whispered temptations and so on). Event text only renders omens and atmosphere and never states the reward, the damage or whether a fight will start, so players have to bet on instinct and on what they know of the carriage's trait; some carriages also branch into side routes with more extreme traits and more focused drops, a detour players take on purpose to build their next loadout.

### Roguelike Meta Progression: Turning Last Run's Lesson Into an Opening Card

Every run — cleared or cut short — settles into experience that raises your **Night Watch level**. What a level unlocks is not a stat bonus but **a card that will be in your hand at the start of the next run**: Hardened Body raises max HP, Killer Instinct raises attack, Night Watch Vigil starts you wearing a gas mask, Provisions and Satchel hand you extra materials, and Fog Scavenger drops a blood crystal straight into your pocket.

Expressing meta progression as cards rather than numbers keeps the fiction and the interface consistent: the summary screen shows you a "newly unlocked starting card", and next run it is literally lying on your table.

### LLM Runtime Content Generation: Creativity to the Model, Rules to the Code

The technical core of this project is the **runtime LLM generation pipeline** for event and enemy cards. Each time you enter a carriage, encounters can be written on the spot by a model instead of drawn from a static pool — but the whole pipeline is tightly constrained:

* **Structural constraints**: JSON only, no explanation; every event must declare a `kind` (combat / normal), and the kind must agree with its description and result — if it reads as a fight, it must actually trigger one. Each batch runs roughly half combat, half normal.
* **Content whitelists**: Icons, grantable items, enemy definition IDs, and behavior IDs all come from whitelists with a compatibility table; loot tables are fixed by code and cannot be chosen by the model.
* **Numeric sovereignty stays in code**: HP and attack written by the model only have to land inside a given tier range — the actual values are rolled by code. **The model is responsible for text quality only.**
* **Prose constraints**: Ornate, oppressive, mysterious, lightly Lovecraftian; period steampunk vocabulary (verdigris, lacquered wood, gaslight, steam, damp, whispers); no modern words or immersion-breaking metaphors; second person; "the anomaly is seen but never explained."
* **Anti-spoiler obfuscation**: Titles and descriptions may only carry atmosphere and omens, never state rewards, damage, or whether combat begins. Combat events must echo the current dungeon's enemy in tone without naming it outright or revealing the outcome.
* **A second proofreading pass**: After generation, a separate "proofreader" prompt checks tone, vagueness, and fit with the enemy's atmosphere, and swaps any drop that duplicates gear already granted this run for one that has not appeared.
* **Context awareness**: Generation carries the player's build tendency (firearm route, light-stacking anti-inhuman, melee blade/blunt, bare fists), current pressure (sustained low HP), recent choices and recent enemies, so the text tracks the actual state of this run.
* **Fallback and caching**: Results feed a cache pool that can be refreshed on demand; on timeout or failure the system silently degrades to the static pool. The player never sees an error — only a slightly more conventional card.
