## My Role

*Night Watch* is an experimental research project I ran at NetEase, exploring **AI-native games** and the **AI game development workflow**: when content production can be handed to a model, what should the gameplay itself look like, and how should the pipeline around it be organized? My work covered:

* **Research and Design of AI-Native Gameplay**: Derived the gameplay structure from the premise that content can be generated by a model at runtime — unifying characters, equipment, materials, locations, events and statuses into cards so new content never needs a new screen, and shaping random encounters into wagers that render atmosphere without stating the outcome, so the text variation a model is best at becomes a playable variable.
* **Core Game Framework Design**: Designed the card data model and the single interaction path — drag into the use slot, then **Craft / Equip / Enter / Use** — and drew the layers and boundaries between combat, equipment slots, damage types and resistances, dungeon progression, and meta progression, so content can keep expanding without touching core logic.
* **LLM Runtime Content Pipeline Design**: Designed the generation protocol for event and enemy cards — JSON-only output, consistency checks between `kind` and the resolved result, whitelists and tier ranges, numbers rolled by code, prose and anti-spoiler constraints, a second proofreading pass, a cache pool and timeout fallback — keeping text creativity with the model and rules and numbers in code.
* **AI Production Pipeline and Integration**: Set the prose tone and art specification, connected the AI generation and human review steps across code, art, music and writing, standardized 80 card illustrations across three resolutions, and integrated the whole content set into a React + Zustand + Three.js front end.

## Project Details

*Night Watch* is a Victorian-gothic **AI-native card roguelike**. Its core idea is to build a card framework that stays intuitive to play while carrying enough systemic depth, and then — through strictly defined content, numeric and rule constraints — let AI-generated content participate reliably in the random expansion that happens inside a run. That gives the game extensive meta progression and in-run build variation, and reaches a genuinely near-infinite space of builds that a traditional roguelike cannot.

The game is built for the web. You play a lamp-carrying watchman who boards a dangerous train and pushes forward through damp corridors, first-class compartments, the dining car, the boiler room and carriages swallowed by fog — gathering materials, crafting gear, adjusting your build and answering encounters, looking for a way to survive one fight after another.

For its gameplay framework, *Night Watch* draws on the card interaction design of *Stacklands* and *Cultist Simulator*, abstracting characters, equipment, materials, locations, events and statuses into a single kind of draggable card object. One operation — dragging a card into the use slot — covers crafting, equipping, entering a location, and using an item. This lowers the cost of understanding the controls while giving content expansion a highly uniform interface: new content needs no new complex screens, only a new card type or new card data plugged into the system.

One note on the playable build: the web version linked at the top of this page runs without the AI backend service deployed, so event and enemy cards are not generated live — they come from a preset static pool. The systems, flow and numbers are all fully playable; only the range of random content stays fixed inside that static pool.

### Everything Is a Card: One Verb, Six Card Types

There is no separate inventory screen, forge screen, or map screen — which action unlocks when a card enters the slot depends only on the card's own type:

* Material → **Craft** (complete a recipe and forge a piece of gear);
* Equipment → **Equip** (into main hand, off hand, head, body, hands, and two trinket slots);
* Location → **Enter** (treat that carriage card as a dungeon run);
* Consumable → **Use** (instant recovery, such as a medkit).

That also lets the tutorial be extremely short: two steps — combine two gears into a gear rod, then equip the rod to your main hand — and the player has learned every control in the game.

### Combat: Every Decision Moves Into Preparation

Combat is auto-resolved turn by turn — equipment applies itself, and the player cannot act mid-fight, only hold to fast-forward. That is not a shortcut but an explicit trade-off: **all decisions are pushed to the moment before you open the carriage door.**

The real play happens in matching damage types against resistances. There are four damage types: blunt, blade, firearm, and light, which is effective against the inhuman. Enemies split into two camps:

* **The human side** (the drenched man, the lurking killer, the armed gunman, the mangled mechanic) — mostly vulnerable to blades and firearms; wet things fear fire, mechanized things scorch.
* **The inhuman side** (whisperers, fog wraiths, the thing that should not exist, the Fog Sovereign) — physical and fog damage barely move them; only light, silver and fire can make them disperse.

Every location card therefore becomes a loadout puzzle. Before an "eldritch whispers" carriage you want the silver cross and the storm lantern; in the machine room the gear rod and pipe wrench serve you better. Bringing the wrong kit is not a mechanical misstep — it decides the fight from round one.

### Crafting, Affixes and Salvage: Making Failure Leave Something Behind

Materials are the blood of this loop. Gears, rusted scrap, leather, gunpowder, gas, blood crystal, silver, cloth, glass, iron ingot, lamp oil, fog-bone ash, fog essence — thirteen materials mapped to carriage traits. Damp zones give leather and scrap, the machine room gives gears, shrines give silver, and only the deepest fog condenses into fog essence.

Crafted gear comes with a **randomly rolled naming prefix affix scaled by rarity**: Keen, Sturdy, Finely Cast, Silver-Inlaid, Fog-Etched, Blood-Soaked. The same recipe never produces exactly the same weapon twice, which keeps "forge another one" worthwhile. Salvage provides the reverse path: gear you no longer need breaks back down into materials for the next craft.

The point is to make **failure accumulate**. A run that ends in the dining car takes no levels with it, but it leaves behind an understanding of recipes, resistances and carriage traits — and a smarter opening hand next time.

### Dungeon Structure: Binary Choices and Hidden Outcomes

Inside a location card, progression means **choosing one of two encounters at every step**. Encounters split into combat and non-combat: scavenging, traps, shrine offerings, the temptation of whispers, a sheltered corner to catch your breath. What the card shows you is only atmosphere.

This is the hard rule I set for event design: **descriptions render omens, smells and sounds only — never what you will receive, how much HP you will lose, or whether a fight starts.** "A dust-covered wooden crate sits in the corner shadow, its brass clasp rusted green" — it might be a medkit, it might be an ambush. The player has to bet on instinct and their read of the carriage trait, not skim a payout and click confirm.

Several carriages branch into side rooms: the machine room off first class, the cargo car off the coupling, the chapel and the fog car off the boiler room. Side rooms carry more extreme traits and more focused drops — detours the player chooses deliberately, for the sake of the next loadout.

### Roguelike Meta Progression: Turning Last Run's Lesson Into an Opening Card

Every run — cleared or cut short — settles into experience that raises your **Night Watch level**. What a level unlocks is not a stat bonus but **a card that will be in your hand at the start of the next run**: Hardened Body raises max HP, Killer Instinct raises attack, Night Watch Vigil starts you wearing a gas mask, Provisions and Satchel hand you extra materials, and Fog Scavenger drops a blood crystal straight into your pocket.

Expressing meta progression as cards rather than numbers keeps the fiction and the interface consistent: the summary screen shows you a "newly unlocked starting card", and next run it is literally lying on your table.

### LLM Runtime Content Generation: Creativity to the Model, Rules to the Code

The technical core of this project is the **runtime LLM generation pipeline** for event and enemy cards. Each time you enter a carriage, encounters can be written on the spot by a model instead of drawn from a static pool — but the whole pipeline is tightly constrained:

* **Structural constraints**: JSON only, no explanation; every event must declare a `kind` (combat / normal), and the kind must agree with its description and result — if it reads as a fight, it must actually trigger one. Each batch runs roughly half combat, half normal.
* **Content whitelists**: Icons, grantable items, enemy definition IDs, and behavior IDs all come from whitelists with a compatibility table; loot tables are fixed by code and cannot be chosen by the model.
* **Numeric sovereignty stays in code**: HP and attack written by the model only have to land inside a given tier range — the actual values are rolled by code. **The model is responsible for text quality only.**
* **Prose constraints**: Ornate, oppressive, mysterious, lightly Lovecraftian; period steampunk vocabulary (verdigris, lacquered wood, gaslight, steam, damp, whispers); no modern words or immersion-breaking metaphors; second person; "the anomaly is seen but never explained."
* **Anti-spoiler obfuscation**: Titles and descriptions may only carry atmosphere and omens, never state rewards, damage, or whether combat begins. Combat events must echo the current dungeon's enemy in tone without naming it outright or revealing the outcome.
* **A second proofreading pass**: After generation, a separate "proofreader" prompt checks tone, vagueness, and fit with the enemy's atmosphere, and swaps any drop that duplicates gear already granted this run for one that has not appeared.
* **Context awareness**: Generation carries the player's build tendency (firearm route, light-stacking anti-inhuman, melee blade/blunt, bare fists), current pressure (sustained low HP), recent choices and recent enemies, so the text tracks the actual state of this run.
* **Fallback and caching**: Results feed a cache pool that can be refreshed on demand; on timeout or failure the system silently degrades to the static pool. The player never sees an error — only a slightly more conventional card.

The pipeline expresses a design position: **an LLM is good at infinite variation of atmosphere and text, and bad at being the referee of a game's rules.** So I placed it on the outermost layer. It can write a thousand versions of "there is a crate in the corner", but what is in the crate, how much HP you lose, and how the fight resolves are always decided by code.

### Presentation Layer and Quality Tiers

The front end uses React + Zustand + Immer for state, with Three.js driving card flips, dealing, and battle camera work. Because browser hardware varies wildly, rendering is split into four quality tiers: **pure 2D table** (3D off, smoothest on low-end machines), **3D cards without battle camera**, **supersampling with halved particles**, and **full 3D with battle camera, supersampling and full particles**. Saves, volume and quality settings persist to localStorage, and an error boundary offers "reset save and reload" instead of stranding the player on a white screen.

For audio, the game carries a fog-night BGM, a set of card and combat sound effects, and three narrated voice lines for the opening epigraph.

### The AI Pipeline: What Was Batch-Generated

Most of the content in the game was batch-generated once the AI workflow was in place:

* **Card art**: 80 card illustrations (12 characters, 24 pieces of equipment, 13 materials, 15 locations, 10 event types, 5 meta progression cards and 1 item), style-unified and exported at three resolutions — 240 asset files in total.
* **Card text**: 27 static event cards; names and entrance narration for 40 enemy preset variants (8 location traits × 5); descriptions for 24 pieces of equipment and 13 materials; 12 prefix affixes; plus the scene prose and win/loss resolution lines for every location.
* **Localization**: two complete text sets, Chinese and English — roughly 340 interface strings plus all card text.
* **Audio**: one fog-night BGM, 8 card and combat sound effects, and 3 narrated lines for the opening epigraph.
* **Code**: the React + Zustand + Immer + Three.js front end, covering card interaction, combat resolution, crafting and salvage, saves, and quality tiers.

My part was to build the skeleton first — card types, damage and resistances, loot tables, location traits, the generation protocol and the prose constraints — then have AI mass-produce content in the shapes that skeleton allows, and review, replace and recycle it piece by piece.
