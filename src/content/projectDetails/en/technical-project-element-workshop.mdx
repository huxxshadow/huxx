## My Role

A solo project: a falling-sand sandbox in which the simulation and the rendering both run entirely on the GPU. The world is a 512 × 288 cellular automaton; every cell is a grain of sand, a drop of water, a wisp of smoke or a piece of a solid body.

- **Simulation**: Cell movement, 44 reactions, heat transfer, phase changes with latent heat, fluid and smoke fields, and rigid bodies, as GPU compute passes. The game does not use Godot's physics.
- **Rendering**: 2D global illumination with Radiance Cascades, light coloured by the matter it passes through, subsurface scattering, and a 2.5D view that gives the flat world depth through lighting.
- **Optimisation**: Through extensive optimisation, cut GPU time per frame to 3.5 ms while keeping every frame byte-identical.

---

## Light Transport

Lighting is 2D global illumination with **Radiance Cascades**. Every cell can emit light (fire, lava, lamps, glowing matter) and every cell attenuates it with an RGB extinction, so light passing through green acid, blue water or red glass is tinted and casts coloured light onto the walls. Translucent materials such as ice, crystal and jelly scatter light inside their bodies (subsurface scattering), with a separate clear-coat layer for sharp highlights.

On top of the global illumination, separate passes trace sun beams through windows, beams from hanging lamps and beams reflected by mirrors; bloom, automatic exposure, AgX tone mapping and a colour-grading LUT finish the image.

## Simulation

Each cell stores its material, temperature, lifetime and flags in one `rgba32ui` texel. Movement uses **Margolus 2 × 2 blocks**: the grid is split into blocks that never overlap, so every thread rearranges its own four cells without write conflicts. Eight sub-steps per tick alternate the block offset. Air is a material too, so sinking and floating follow the same density comparison for powders, liquids and gases.

Reactions come from a 256 × 256 lookup table of reactant pairs with probabilities and temperature windows, evaluated for neighbouring pairs inside each block. Heat diffuses between cells; phase changes absorb or release latent heat. A quarter-resolution fluid solver adds hot-air buoyancy and blast waves, and a half-resolution medium field carries smoke, steam and flame.

## Fire and Explosions

Flammable materials ignite above their ignition temperature and spread fire to their neighbours. Flames feed the medium field, which glows, rises with the fluid solver and lights the scene. Explosions write pressure into the fluid field, so the blast pushes powders, liquids and loose bodies outward.

## 2.5D View

Shapes stay two-dimensional; depth comes from lighting. The final image is composed by one view shader that marches each pixel's ray through the playable layer, treated as a slab a few cells thick, and then through a background relief made of windows, pillars and rock walls:

- **Relief normals**: Outlines lean towards or away from the light, so edges catch light like carved relief.
- **Interfaces**: Rays bend at water, glass and ice according to their refractive index; Fresnel reflections are traced back through the scene.
- **Background**: The relief is lit by depth-blurred light, with shadows cast by the foreground, caustics behind water and contact occlusion.
- **Atmosphere**: Haze between the layers, aerial perspective, a day–night cycle and a slight parallax when the camera moves.

## Performance

The frame budget is 16 ms (60 fps). The lighting effects went in one at a time and each one made the frame slower, so optimisation ran through the whole project instead of happening once at the end. The steps below are in the order they happened (RTX 5090, 512 × 288 world).

### Step 1: See where every millisecond goes

First came an in-game performance panel (F3):

- **Per-pass timings**: GPU milliseconds for every render pass, shown as bars.
- **Cost heat map**: the 2.5D view shader counts its samples per pixel for the background, the air and the foreground, overlays them on the picture and converts them into milliseconds per material, so the most expensive material is visible at a glance.
- **Per-effect cost**: each effect is briefly switched off in turn, and the on/off difference gives the GPU time it really costs.

### Step 2: Move per-pixel work to world resolution

The heat map showed the foreground volumetric fog as the most expensive part: it ray-marched with dithering on every pixel of the final picture. Fog and light shafts are smooth and do not need that resolution, so the fog is now precomputed into a texture at the 512 × 288 world resolution and sampled bilinearly when the picture is composited. Compositing dropped from about 1.0 ms to 0.13 ms, the light gallery from 1.88 ms to 0.93 ms per frame, and the dithering noise went away with it.

### Step 3: Beams at half resolution

Once reflected and refracted beams were in, they became the new largest cost. A beam deposits its energy into a buffer with atomic adds along its path, and contention on those atomic writes was the main expense:

- the accumulation buffer went to half resolution;
- a beam deposits every second step and no longer splits each deposit bilinearly over four cells, which leaves about 1/8 of the atomic writes;
- the blur that follows is a 3 × 3 at half resolution.

Optics bench 1.58 → 1.05 ms per frame, light gallery 1.29 → 0.94 ms, lab 1.08 → 0.86 ms. Lamp beams also got a cheap early test: probe 56 cells in a straight line first and skip the ray if nothing transparent or mirror-like lies ahead; split into wavelengths only on entering dispersive matter, and trace a single white ray otherwise.

### Step 4: A regression test that guards the picture

Sunlight wavefronts and 8-band spectral dispersion came next, and daytime scenes grew to 5.5–7.9 ms, with beams alone taking over 3.4 ms. Going further meant changing how the shaders execute, which must not break the picture.

At a fixed frame rate the game is deterministic (the simulation's random numbers are seeded by the tick), so an optimisation that keeps the result has to reproduce the original picture exactly. `perf_check.py` renders 9 scenes (the maps with the heaviest lighting, a fire scene and the scripted demos) for 240 frames each at a fixed time step, averages the per-pass timings over the second half, and compares every screenshot **byte for byte** with a reference captured before the change. Any differing pixel is reported with the largest error; only changes that match everywhere are kept.

### Step 5: Beams from serial to parallel

The per-pass timings pointed at two serial chains:

- **8 wavelengths one after another**: as soon as one ray in a group touched dispersive glass, the whole group traced all 8 wavelengths in sequence. Now each wavelength gets its own layer of workgroups (`gl_WorkGroupID.z`) and the 8 layers run side by side. Deposits are integer atomic adds, which do not depend on order, so the result is bit-identical.
- **The sun probe was the longest serial chain of the frame**: each of the 640 sunlight rays was walked by one thread across about 600 cells to find the first matter that redirects light. The new `sun_probe.comp` cuts every ray into 32-step segments probed by one thread each; a hit is encoded as `0x7FFFFFFF − t` and `atomicMax` keeps the earliest one, which is exactly the answer of walking the ray from its start.

| Scene | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stained-glass hall (cover) | 7.9 ms | 3.7 ms |
| Optics bench | 7.2 ms | 3.4 ms |
| Light gallery | 6.8 ms | 3.3 ms |
| Lab | 5.4 ms | 3.1 ms |

### Step 6: The simulation in shared memory

- **Lateral liquid spread**: liquid moves while a row is being scanned, so the scan has to be serial. Each thread used to read and write the global image cell by cell. Now one workgroup handles each 64-cell row segment: its 64 threads load the segment and the cells supporting it from below into shared memory in parallel, one thread runs the same serial scan there, and the whole group writes it back. 0.18–0.25 ms → 0.05 ms.
- **Pressure solve**: fluid pressure is solved with Jacobi iterations of a Poisson equation, which used to take one dispatch per iteration, 20 in all. The 128 × 72 pressure grid fits in one workgroup's shared memory, so a single 1024-thread workgroup now runs every iteration there with precomputed neighbour masks; the sums are taken in the same order, so the result is identical. 0.18 ms → 0.09 ms. Larger grids keep the old path.
- **Idle pass**: on ticks without player input the command pass only copied the grid, so it is skipped.

The simulation became twice as fast, again with byte-identical output.

### Result

In the heaviest scene at 1536 × 864, GPU time per frame went from **7.9 ms to 3.5 ms**, and the pictures of all 9 scenes are byte-identical to those before the optimisations.
